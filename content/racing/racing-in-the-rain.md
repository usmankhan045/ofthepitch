British racing runs in the rain. Meetings are rarely abandoned for weather alone, and the ground getting soft changes the racing itself rather than cancelling it. What rain does change is your day, and most of what goes wrong is footwear.

Here is what actually happens when the weather turns at a British racecourse.

## Do race meetings get cancelled for rain?

Rarely for rain by itself. Racing is an outdoor winter and summer sport in a country where it rains, and the fixtures are built for it.

Abandonment usually follows waterlogging, where the ground cannot absorb any more and becomes unsafe, or frost and snow in winter. Ascot publishes refund arrangements covering admission and car parking in defined circumstances if racing is abandoned, which tells you it happens rarely enough to be a specific policy rather than a routine one.

Individual races can also be affected while the meeting continues. Horses are withdrawn when the ground no longer suits them, which is why the going is discussed so constantly.

## What does the going mean when it rains?

The going is the state of the ground, and rain moves it toward soft and heavy.

The scale runs roughly from firm through good to heavy, with soft in between. Firm ground is fast and suits quick horses. Heavy ground is demanding and suits horses with stamina, and times are slower.

This is not a technicality for spectators. It is why a favourite can be beaten, why trainers withdraw horses on the morning of a race, and why the result on a wet day often differs from what the form suggested. Both Ascot and the Jockey Club publish the going for their meetings, and it updates as conditions change.

If you have backed a horse and the going changes overnight, that is a real event, not a detail.

## What should you wear to the races in the rain?

Layers and footwear you can walk on wet grass in. That is most of it.

Cheltenham's own dress code page carries the most specific advice published by any British racecourse on this, from Holland Cooper founder Jade Holland Cooper, whose brand works with the Festival. Her recommendations:

- **Layer up.** Tights under trousers, thermals, a thin roll neck under a roll neck.
- **Gloves.** She names them as the accessory she cannot do without at the races, because it is always colder than people expect.
- **Sensible shoes**, because you are on your feet and moving around the course all day.

What to avoid, in the same guidance: bare legs, floaty skirts and thin materials. Her summary is that a wet raceday is about outerwear, not the outfit underneath it.

Cheltenham itself advises checking the forecast and warns that although most car parks have been laid with trackway, it is still a walk to the racecourse, so footwear should suit the conditions.

## Can you wear wellies to the races?

At most racecourses, yes. Cheltenham, Aintree and Epsom have no formal dress code, so footwear is your choice and wellington boots are common at winter jump meetings.

Royal Ascot is the exception. Trainers are banned site wide, and the Royal Enclosure specifies black dress shoes for men. Ascot does not publish a wellington boot rule, but the Royal and Queen Anne Enclosures are formal enough that boots would be out of place even where not explicitly prohibited.

The practical answer at Ascot in wet weather is a shoe you can walk on grass in that still meets the enclosure's code.

## Where can you shelter at a racecourse?

Less than you would hope, and it depends heavily on your enclosure.

Grandstands offer cover, but reaching them requires a ticket that includes them. At Royal Ascot the Queen Anne Enclosure includes the ground floor of the Grandstand and the steppings; the Windsor and Village Enclosures have no Grandstand access at all.

Some enclosures are specifically described as partially covered. Aintree's Princess Royal Roof is a partially covered standing area, and its Lord Sefton Lower includes a partially covered private bar.

If you are buying a ticket for a winter meeting and the weather matters to you, look for the word covered in the enclosure description rather than assuming shelter exists.

Cloakrooms are available at Royal Ascot in every enclosure from 10.30am, so a coat can be left once weather clears rather than carried all day.

## Does rain affect getting there?

Mainly through car parks. Racecourse parking is on grass at most venues, and heavy rain makes both the walk and the exit slower.

Cheltenham warns specifically that although trackway has been laid in most car parks, it is still a walk to the racecourse. At Royal Ascot, parking must be pre-booked and costs £45, so arriving to find conditions poor is not a decision you can reverse on the day.

Trains avoid the problem. South Western Railway runs to Ascot from London Waterloo in about 52 minutes, and the station is a seven minute walk from the racecourse. Note that Oyster and contactless are not valid to Ascot, so a paper ticket is needed.

## Should you still go if the forecast is bad?

Usually yes, with two caveats.

Go if you have dressed for it. A wet raceday with the right coat and boots is a perfectly good day, and the crowd is smaller and more relaxed than at a sold out summer meeting.

Reconsider if you are in an enclosure with no cover and no way to reach one, or if you have booked something with no refund and the forecast suggests abandonment rather than just rain. Ascot cannot offer refunds for non attendance, only in defined circumstances where racing is abandoned.

The people who have a bad day in the rain are almost always the ones who dressed for photographs rather than for weather.

## The short version

Rain rarely stops racing. It changes the going, which changes the racing, which is part of the sport rather than a problem with it.

Wear boots you can walk in, take a coat that actually keeps water out, layer underneath, and check whether your enclosure has any cover before you buy it. Everything else takes care of itself.

**Sources:** Going, abandonment and refund policies, enclosure cover and travel details are published by Ascot at ascot.com and the Jockey Club at thejockeyclub.co.uk, checked in September 2026. The layering and footwear guidance is from the Cheltenham Festival's own dress code page.
