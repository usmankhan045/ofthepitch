Yes, you can go to the races knowing nothing about horse racing, and you will probably enjoy it. Most of the crowd at any British race meeting knows less than they appear to, and the parts of the day that matter most have nothing to do with form.

This is the honest version of what to expect if you have never been and do not follow the sport.

## Will people know you have never been to the races?

No. There is no way to tell, and nobody is looking.

The things that would give you away are all invisible: you do not need to bet, you do not need to read a racecard, and you will not be asked anything by anyone. The staff take your ticket, and after that you are just a person standing outside on a nice afternoon.

The only visible thing is what you wear, and at three of the four biggest British meetings there is no dress code at all.

## Do you need to bet at the races?

No. Nothing about a raceday requires it and plenty of people never place one.

If you want to try, the practical approach is to treat a small stake as the price of caring about one race rather than as an investment. Pick a horse, put a few pounds on it, watch it. That is genuinely what most casual racegoers are doing.

If you would rather not bet at all, pick a horse by its name or by the jockey's colours. It does exactly the same job of giving you a reason to watch.

## How do you follow a race if you know nothing?

By the colours. The silks a jockey wears identify the owner, and they are the only reliable way to pick out one horse from another at distance.

Find your horse's colours on the racecard before the race, then watch for them. Trying to follow the whole field is what makes racing feel impenetrable, and even people who follow the sport do not do it.

The commentator will call the leaders. If your colours are not mentioned, your horse is not near the front, which tells you what you need to know.

## What does a day at the races actually involve?

Far more standing around than racing. At Royal Ascot gates open at 10.30am, the first race is at 2.30pm and the last starts at 6.10pm. Races are roughly half an hour apart and each takes a few minutes.

So the racing occupies perhaps twenty minutes of a day that runs six or seven hours. The rest is walking, eating, drinking and talking outdoors.

Once you understand that ratio, the day makes sense. It is an outdoor social event with racing punctuating it, not a sporting fixture you watch continuously.

## What if you do not understand the commentary?

You will understand most of it, and the rest does not matter.

Three terms carry most of the meaning. **The going** is the state of the ground, which affects which horses run well. A **furlong** is an eighth of a mile, used to count down the finish. A **handicap** is a race where better horses carry more weight, which is why favourites lose.

Everything else is names of horses and names of races. You do not need to know them, any more than you need to know the players to enjoy a football match in a stadium.

## What should you wear if you know nothing about racing?

Whatever you are comfortable standing in for a day outdoors, unless you are going to Royal Ascot.

Cheltenham, Aintree and Epsom all publish guidance saying there is no formal dress code, and Aintree and Epsom both name jeans and clean trainers as a perfectly good choice. Royal Ascot is the exception, with dress codes that vary by enclosure and site wide bans on trainers, denim, leggings and shorts.

The universal advice, whatever the meeting: shoes you can stand in for six hours on grass, and a layer for weather that changes.

## Is it awkward going to the races alone?

Less than most sporting events, because so much of the day is spent moving around rather than sitting in an assigned seat.

Racecourse seating is generally unreserved, so there is no obvious empty chair beside you. You can walk to the rail for a race, get food between races, and leave when you want.

## What is the one thing worth knowing beforehand?

Which enclosure your ticket is for and what it includes, because that decides your day more than anything else.

Specifically: whether it includes Parade Ring access, where the horses are walked before each race. At Royal Ascot only the Royal and Queen Anne Enclosures have it. The Village and Windsor Enclosures do not.

For someone new to racing, seeing the horses up close is often the best part of the day, and it is entirely possible to buy a ticket that does not allow it.

## What is the Parade Ring and why does it matter?

An area where the horses are walked in circles before each race so people can look at them, with jockeys joining them there before going out to the track.

It matters for a first visit because it is where racing stops being abstract. A racehorse thirty feet away is a genuinely impressive animal in a way that a field of horses at distance is not.

If you are choosing between two tickets and one includes Parade Ring access, that is the one to buy.

## Will you be bored?

If you expect continuous action, possibly. If you expect a long afternoon outdoors with things happening periodically, no.

The people who do not enjoy racedays are usually the ones who came expecting a sporting event and found a social one. The people who do enjoy them treat it as a day out that has racing in it.

Go with that expectation and knowing nothing about racing genuinely does not matter.
